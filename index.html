What this tool shows

A GC detector turns the compounds leaving the column into an electrical signal, and each type responds to something different. Choose a detector and watch which compounds show up, how big their peaks are, and how the carrier gas and flow change things.

FID: counts carbon

Compounds burn in a small hydrogen–air flame. Carbon atoms make CHO⁺ ions, and the current collected is roughly proportional to the number of carbons per second (the effective carbon number; carbons bonded to O or Cl count less). Water, CO₂, N₂ and CCl₄ give little or nothing. The FID is a mass-flow-sensitive detector: it responds to grams per second, so peak height follows the flow while peak area doesn’t change.

TCD: universal, but less sensitive

A heated filament loses heat to the gas around it. Helium and hydrogen conduct heat far better than organic vapors, so when an analyte arrives the filament gets hotter. The signal is proportional to the concentration times the difference in thermal conductivity, so with a nitrogen carrier the response is small, and compounds that conduct better than N₂ (like methane) even give negative peaks. The TCD is concentration sensitive: peak height stays the same when the flow changes, but peak area falls as flow rises.

ECD: selective for halogens

A ⁶³Ni foil emits β electrons that create a steady current. Electronegative molecules, especially polyhalogenated ones like CCl₄, capture electrons and reduce the current. It is extremely sensitive to them and nearly blind to hydrocarbons, but it saturates at high amounts (a small linear range).

Sensitivity and detection limits

A peak is detected when its height is about three times the noise (S/N ≥ 3). The limit of detection is LOD ≈ 3·noise/sensitivity. Use the amount slider to find each detector’s detection limit for hexane or CCl₄.

Thermal conductivities, effective carbon numbers and capture coefficients are approximate, chosen to show realistic trends; retention times are illustrative for a test mixture. FID sensitivity 0.015 C/g of carbon; noise levels are typical values.
